Gen11 Solar Car Electrical System

Systems Engineering • Electrical Architecture • Verification and Integration
2025-26

🚧 This project is actively being worked on. More documentation, images, and technical details will be added soon.

New Power Architecture

The main flaw in the 10th generations power architecture was in car shutoff edge cases. Mainly a chicken and the egg problem where we needed the car to tell the car it shut off when it shuts off. To do this we reimplemented power distribution with a LVPDB (Low Voltage Power Distribution Board). The board contains a switching converter to step HV battery to a 12V line and more importantly logic on how to distribute the 12V. Our solution was to add an aux pack to power the car for a bit when the car is in "shut down mode" instead of no power. This allows CAN to still have power and boards to still be able to communicate. The logic for not draining the aux is a simple power priority IC that prioritizes battery 12V at all times if it exists. In general, this fixes the problem of boards like strobe not knowing to get triggered when the car is abruptly stopped from, for example, EStop.

We also had a reoccuring problems of our contactors to the solar array getting welded together. This was due to us having an RC precharge circuit for the motors, but not the array. In turn, we revised the HVPDB to add another precharge circuit.

Current Progress

I am currently spending a lot of time researching motors and motor controllers, and trying to get past generation motors to spin with various controllers. The process is mainly a lot of datasheet reading and documentation of motor and motor controller behaviour in different conditions. There has also been a lot of harnessing to create wire adaptors between different kinds of connectors.

We are also redoing a few low voltage boards for either greater functionality or better ergonomics, and switching the MCU we are using on all boards to the ESP32 Wroom. One board being redone is steering, as it has been notoriously not ergonomic as there was a rats nest of wires in the steering wheel, and we needed to take apart the whole wheel to flash new code or snoop the serial monitor. I am also pushing for a new sensors project to give more data to our strategy team. I am also working closely with the BMS project to push out a more reliable battery protection system.

I have also done a lot of thinking and planning for enclosures and harnessing. We are planning to work with our composites team for custom composite boxes for each board. We are also working with our new sponsor EPlan that I helped get in order to plan harnessing in a software before we implement it on an actual car.

CALICO Informatics Competition Fa25

Software • Algorithms • Event Planning • Scripting • Data Pipelining
Fall 2025 GitHub

Contest Summary

CALICO Fall 2025 consisted of 12 problems and 17 total test sets. These problems are designed to range from Rank 1, easy enough for anyone who has taken any programming lesson ever to code, to Rank 4, hard enough to stump even world class programmers. I personally oversaw the creation of six of the problems, while onboarding members and editing the other problems. The leaderboard is shown here

The competition itself had 2000+ registrations total, with 60+ coming in person to compete, including the famous competitive programmer tourist. This continues our trend of the contest growing every semester.

The Problems I Wrote

The first problem I wrote was a cute beginner problem called gta6. The entire problem was given a date, return whether the event happens before or after the release of gta6. This problem along with the second problem bizzfuzz (basically asking given the last two words said in the game of bizzfuzz, output the next word to say), were used as tools for me to help onboard new members into our problem writing process. This onboarding includes teaching how to interface with our problem statements on LaTeX, teaching about our library calico_lib and how it eases the creation of test cases for problems, and teaching how our online problem judging on CALICOJudge work.

The next problem I wrote was up, which is given a X-Z plane projection of a 3D shape and each squares depth, find the minimal area of the shapes projection in the X-Y plane. This problem in general was also on the easier side (being rank 1), so the only interesting note is that we were strapped for time on this problem and finalized most of the problem at the last day before the contest.

The 4th problem of the contest and the first rank 2 is a problem called chess, where you are given the x, y location of chess pieces and you must find the number of instances of pieces that are attacking other pieces. I thought this problem was cute, as we allowed for the problem to have a bonus test set allowing naive solutions to pass through. More specifically, you could get less points with an O(N^2) algorithm that naively pairwise compares pieces which is more suitable to beginners. However, you can get more points with an O(NlogN) solution that cleverly book keeps pieces as 4 unique intercepts for the 4 directions a piece can move (vertical, horizontal, and both diagonals) and lets you sort and parse the values. We did find in practice it was a bit of an implementation hell problem, but it was still interesting for the more beginner problem solvers.

The 5th problem of the contest and the second rank 2 is a problem called decibel, where you are given a string and a number of amplifications, and you must output the score of amplifying the string k times. The score is a-z (1-26) and A-Z (27-52), with amplifications being a->A, A-Aa. This problem also has two solutions, naive and smart. The naive is an exponential time simulation, that is simply there to teach solvers how to do string manipulation. The reason this problem was pushed however is the smart solution which we thought was clever. If you watch the growth of the amplification of each character, you can notice the string grows exponentially by the fibonacci recurrence relation. This means the solution is to calculate fib in linear time (by whatever means, such as DP or just an array) and multiply each letter my that value smartly. Generally contestants enjoyed it, and I think this was one of the smarter problems we designed.

The final problem I personally designed was a rank 2+3+4, meaning a lot of variation. The problem, with me simplifying the premise and loading of the data structure, is that you are given a tree with different items at each node, find the number of items you can grab on your way to depth K. The unique part however, is that certain nodes are portals that contain a copy of the entire tree, effectively creating an infinitely and exponentially big tree. Because loading in the tree is a challenge in itself, we allow a naive BFS as our rank 2 solution. However, when playing around with this idea we had the feeling there was a more close formed solution you can find once you reach the lowest portal. We then realized this can be written as a linear recurrence relation, with each difference equation referring to the depth relative to the top layer. This allowed for our rank 3 solution to be matrix exponentiation on this recurrence relation. The rank 4 solution funny enough was only discovered when we started verifying the problem. We assumed that recurrence relations couldn't be solved faster, but someone on the team found this technique for linear recurrence relations called the Kitamasa Method that uses FFT and polynomial multiplication to solve the relation faster. As a side note, I found this solution very coincidentally funny as me and the other problem writer who wrote this problem are in a signals and systems class that teaches very similar concepts to this problems solution.

The Infastructure

The bulk of our contestant data infastructure is managed by google sheets and python scripts. We effectively use an ETL pipeline which goes as follows: extract, we take data from google forms; transform, we parse the data and format it; load, we automatically generate things such as certificates, login information, and raffle IDs.

Our actual contest infastructure i haven't touched too much, but it runs on an API of DOMJudge, which we reformated and called CALICOJudge. The main thing I interface with is the judge hosts and they work with problems. This is mainly just making sure uploading problems is elegant so testing is more seamless.

Car Horn PCB

Mixed-signal audio PCB with custom ESP32 dev board, CAN integration, and I²C DAC chain.

Roles: PCB design • Firmware • Bring-up • Signal validation
Oct 2024 – Apr 2025

"Solar cars shall be equipped with a horn that can be heard at a sound power level between 75 and 102 dBA at a distance of 15 m in front of the solar car. The horn shall be permanently mounted, operated from the steering wheel, and shall be able to operate for up to 5 minutes continuously at the required volume."

ESP32 Dev Board

The first major sub circuit is the ESP32 Dev Board. It is implemented with an ESP32 Wroom 1 and necessary peripherals including boot and reset buttons, and a USB to UART IC. The design process came mainly from reading the ESP and UART IC datasheets and picking correct passives around them. The layout was tricky due to needing differential routing for the UART and USB differential lines. Effort was also taken to deal with large current draw from the horn by having large polygon power planes around the amplifier. 2nd Layer was also used as a ground plane for similar reasons. There was also a large effort by me to make the boards layout modular and readable through verbose silkscreen and documentation of each sub circuit on confluence.

Power

The board has the challenge of using 12V CAN BUS power for most LV operations, but requiring an 18V aux pack for the higher current draw amplifier. This is achieved through two diode OR circuits. There is one for LV between the 12V CAN line (that comes from a differential five pin molex connector to the car) and the 5V USB power. This diode OR goes into a 3.3V buck converter, that was chosen to allow flexibility with 12V and 24V CAN lines, as well as interfacing with the 5V USB. The other OR circuit is between the 18V and 12V circuit, with a priority circuit using FETs that chooses the 18V power. This allows for a nominal 18V power on the amplifier, with a redundant 12V in case the aux pack dies. The biggest design problems were the general high power draw of the horn, that required a lot of thermal considerations and IC choice.

Sound

The sound architecture goes: ESP digital IO pin -> I2C based DAC -> High gain amplifier -> Speaker. Between the digital pins and the DAC there is open drain architecture for data and clock. The output of the DAC has heavy decoupling capacitors to smooth output, as well as a LP filter to attenuate high frequencies. The amp only takes positive voltage, so the output sine wave must be biased to 1.65 volts before going into the amp. After the amp, we chose a large capacitor in series with the speaker to cut the DC bias. The hardest design decisions were finding a DAC, amplifier, and speaker setup that interfaced with each other and the given power constraints. This was solved with hand calculations for gain as well as breadboarding and simulations with SPICE.

Bring Up + Verification

The board in the image is the 2nd iteration of the board. During the first bring up we noticed two things while bench testing. One, we greatly underestimated the power draw of the amplifier relative to the trace width and heat sinks we had. Second, we found a data sheet misread of the UART IC leading to us needing to jump D+ and D- with microwire. Both we fixed in the second iteration. The bring up process for the second draft was done mainly with: DC power supplies and multimeters to verify power circuitry; waveform generators to verify the amplifier; oscilloscopes to verify the digital and analog signals coming in and out of the DAC; and CAN snooping combined with Serial monitoring to debug firmware. We however did have a shorted trace caused from a polygon and trace being too big, leading us to cut it with a knife. In the end, the horn worked with a few microwire jumps and traces cut.

Firmware

The firmware for the most part is simple, relying heavily on datasheet reading and use of the I2C interface. The main interesting thing was to figure out how to create a digital sound signal to output. We effectively took layered sine waves representing the signal we wanted to output and summed the amplitude at sampled points. We then put it into an array and iterated through the array to output the value. Otherwise, we had to interface into the CAN interface of the rest of the car. This includes reading data and looking for a signal from steering to power on the horn, and sending a heartbeat on CAN to the rest of the car to signal the horn boards microcontroller is still operational.

CalSol Electrical Onboarding

Open-source onboarding curriculum to teach PCB design & electrical fundamentals

Education • PCB Design • Open Source • Teaching
2025 GitHub

The CalSol Electrical Onboarding project is an open-source educational repository developed for the CalSol team to onboard new members in PCB design and basic Electrical Engineering. It guides learners through schematic design, PCB layout, firmware writing, and practical Electrical Engineering skills, with a hands-on project that culminates in the design and bring up of a PWM Generator. It is also designed to be fast, being done in two weeks. In turn, this onboarding gives a taste of Electrical Engineering before throwing members onto a project.

Curriculum & Pedagogy

The onboarding curriculum is modular: learners first learn about the components and concepts required to understand a PWM generator (including various passives and ICs); then they learn about the KiCAD software to perform PCB schematic and PCB layout; they then do physical board bring up and verification with an oscilloscope; and capped off with a firmware primer. Each module includes dense tutorials, project guidelines, and exercises to reinforce best practices. The goal is to prime people illiterate in Electrical Engineering into a position that they can put in valuable work on any PCB project. This is why the pedagogy is letting each person get there hands on the full life cycle of a project, with the onboarding staff acting to purely assist the new members.

The Project

The project is a PWM motor generator. This project was selected as it hit a few major requirements. These include: use of many components including passives, diodes and MOSFETs; existence of different waveform signals including a square wave, sawtooth wave, and triangle wave; the need to read at least one IC datasheet; and enough components to make layout interesting. This allows us to give a glimpse of Electrical Engineering to the person reading through the lab. The firmware project is also very basic, being a button that triggers the ESP32 to output a similar PWM signal.

Outcomes & Impact

New CalSol members are able to join projects immediately, having completed a real PCB design exercise by the end of the onboarding. The exposure to everything makes the job of project leads easier, leading to seamless transition into projects. The open-source nature also allows other students and student groups to use or adapt our project to further teach engineering. We have on many occasions referred interested students to the project to make Berkeley more PCB literate.

Guitar Pedals

Op-amp and BJT based guitar pedals for distortion and filtering

Skills: Bandpass Filter, Op Amp, BJT
Mar 2025 – Present

Op-Amp Circuit

The pedal circuit is centered around a bandpass filter going through an op-amp in order to shape and amplify the guitar signal. The op amp is in the non-inverting configuration with a varaible resistor to determine the feedback, therefore controlling the gain. There is a HP filter before and after the op amp to attenutate DC signals and to boost treble frequencies. There is a LP filter in parallel with the potentiometer to create a desired gain within the bandwidth. The result is a treble boosting circuit that attenuates signals outside of the desired treble range.

Simulation & Testing

Each filter of the pedal was simulated in LTspice to validate frequency response, stability, and expected bode plot behavior. Frequency analysis confirmed the bandpass characteristics and the correct cutoff frequencies. I observed the time domain plots as well to validate the phase didn't flip to -180 degrees. Both time and frequency domain analysis allowed me to verify that the passives I chose were the correct values in order to achieve the correct bandwidth and gain. I further verified on a breadboard using an oscilloscope and waveform generators to simulate different frequency signals, in order to find a frequency response. I finalized the project by plugging in my guitar input into the circuit input, and the output into my amplifier to verify the correct output sound.

BJT Circuit

I am currently applying what I've learned about BJT linear amplifiers from EE105 to design a guitar pedal that implements a distortion sound effect. I am trying to abuse the physical sagging of the gain caused by the BJT to create a clipped sound. I am currently trying to tweak different resistor and capacitor values through simulation (LTSpice) and through physical testing (breadboard), in order to get a desired tone (through signal clipping), and bandwidth (through the surrounding passives).
